# ABE Accelerated Economics Reference

This reference is a compact concept map for an accelerated beginning economics course for MBA students. Use it to supplement uploaded exams and solutions. Uploaded course materials remain the primary source for wording, emphasis, and local conventions.

## 1. Economic way of thinking

- Scarcity means resources are limited relative to wants. Every choice has an opportunity cost: the highest-valued alternative forgone.
- Marginal thinking compares the additional benefit and additional cost of one more unit. Continue an activity while marginal benefit is at least marginal cost.
- Positive statements are testable claims about what is or was. Normative statements express a value judgment about what ought to be.
- Ceteris paribus means other relevant factors are held constant.
- Incentives change behavior. Sunk costs are already incurred and should not affect a forward-looking marginal decision.

## 2. Production possibilities and trade

- A production possibility frontier shows maximum attainable combinations of two outputs with current resources and technology.
- Points on the frontier are productively efficient; points inside are attainable but inefficient; points outside are unattainable without growth or trade.
- The slope of the PPF is the marginal rate of transformation and represents opportunity cost.
- A bowed-out PPF represents increasing opportunity cost. A straight PPF represents constant opportunity cost.
- Economic growth or a productivity improvement shifts the frontier outward. A disaster or loss of resources shifts it inward.
- Comparative advantage is the lower opportunity cost, not necessarily the greater absolute output. Specialization and voluntary trade can make both parties better off when trade occurs within mutually beneficial terms.

## 3. Supply, demand, and equilibrium

- Demand is the relationship between price and quantity demanded, holding other determinants fixed. A price change causes movement along demand; income, preferences, related-good prices, expectations, and number of buyers shift demand.
- Supply is the relationship between price and quantity supplied. Input prices, technology, taxes, expectations, number of sellers, and regulations can shift supply.
- Equilibrium occurs where quantity demanded equals quantity supplied. A binding price ceiling creates a shortage; a binding price floor creates a surplus.
- A demand increase usually raises equilibrium price and quantity with upward-sloping supply. A supply increase usually lowers price and raises quantity.
- Consumer surplus is willingness to pay minus price. Producer surplus is price minus minimum willingness to accept or marginal cost. Total surplus is maximized in the competitive equilibrium under standard assumptions.
- A per-unit tax creates a wedge between buyer and seller prices, reduces quantity traded, and can create deadweight loss. Tax incidence depends on relative elasticities, not on which side legally remits the tax.

## 4. Elasticity and revenue

- Price elasticity of demand = percentage change in quantity demanded divided by percentage change in price. Use the absolute value for elastic, inelastic, or unit elastic classifications.
- Elastic demand has magnitude greater than 1; inelastic demand has magnitude less than 1; unit elastic demand has magnitude equal to 1.
- Total revenue = price times quantity. With inelastic demand, a price increase raises total revenue. With elastic demand, a price increase lowers total revenue. At unit elasticity, total revenue is locally maximized.
- Demand is more elastic when close substitutes exist, the good is a larger budget share, consumers have more time to adjust, or the good is narrowly defined.
- For a monopolist, marginal revenue is below demand for positive quantities. Profit maximization occurs where MR = MC, followed by charging the price on the demand curve at that output.

## 5. Production and costs

- In the short run at least one input is fixed. Total fixed cost does not change with output; total variable cost changes with output; total cost = TFC + TVC.
- TFC equals total cost at zero output. TVC = TC - TFC. Average fixed cost = TFC/Q. Average variable cost = TVC/Q. Average total cost = TC/Q = AFC + AVC.
- Marginal cost = change in total cost divided by change in output: MC = delta TC / delta Q. It is not total cost divided by output.
- Diminishing marginal product means additional variable input eventually adds less output, which tends to make marginal cost rise.
- The shutdown rule for a competitive firm in the short run is to produce if price covers minimum AVC; the break-even rule uses minimum ATC. A price-taking firm produces where P = MR = MC on the relevant rising portion of MC.
- Economies of scale describe long-run average cost falling as output expands; diseconomies describe long-run average cost rising.

## 6. Labor and resource markets

- Labor demand is derived demand: firms hire because labor contributes to revenue. In a competitive output market, marginal revenue product is price times marginal physical product.
- Labor market equilibrium determines the wage and employment. A binding minimum wage can create a surplus of labor when set above equilibrium.
- Human capital includes education, training, skills, and experience that raise productivity. Firms may pay for training when they expect to recover the investment through higher productivity or retention.
- Frictional unemployment comes from job search; structural unemployment comes from mismatch; cyclical unemployment comes from insufficient aggregate demand. The natural rate includes frictional and structural unemployment in the standard model.

## 7. GDP, growth, and living standards

- GDP is the market value of final goods and services produced within a country during a period. It can be measured as Y = C + I + G + (X - M).
- Consumption is household spending, investment is spending on new productive capital and structures, government purchases exclude transfer payments, exports are added, and imports are subtracted to remove foreign production.
- Nominal GDP uses current prices. Real GDP uses constant or base-year prices and is better for comparing production over time. GDP deflator = nominal GDP / real GDP times 100.
- GDP is not a complete measure of welfare: it omits or poorly captures distribution, unpaid work, environmental damage, leisure, health, and quality of life.
- Long-run growth depends on productivity, human capital, physical capital, institutions, technology, and available resources.

## 8. Inflation and unemployment

- Inflation is a sustained increase in the general price level, not merely a one-time increase in one price. The inflation rate is the percentage change in a price index.
- CPI tracks a representative consumer basket. The GDP deflator covers final domestic production and changes with the composition of output.
- Unexpected inflation redistributes between borrowers and lenders and can distort decisions. Real values remove the effect of price changes; approximately, real interest rate = nominal interest rate - expected inflation.
- Unemployment rate = unemployed / labor force. Labor force = employed + unemployed people actively seeking work. People not working and not seeking work are outside the labor force.

## 9. Aggregate demand, supply, and policy

- Aggregate demand represents planned spending and slopes downward in the price-level/output model. Its components are consumption, investment, government purchases, and net exports.
- Short-run aggregate supply is upward sloping when wages and input prices are sticky. Long-run aggregate supply is vertical at potential output in the standard model.
- A negative demand shock lowers output and the price level in the short run. An adverse supply shock lowers output and raises the price level, creating stagflation pressure.
- Expansionary fiscal policy raises government purchases or lowers taxes. Contractionary fiscal policy does the opposite. Monetary policy works through money, interest rates, credit, and aggregate demand.
- Stabilization policy has trade-offs, implementation lags, and possible crowding out. Long-run growth policy should be distinguished from short-run demand management.

## 10. Market structures, public policy, and externalities

- Perfect competition has many firms, standardized products, free entry, and price-taking behavior. Monopoly has a single seller and barriers to entry. Oligopoly has a small number of strategically interdependent firms.
- Market power allows a firm to restrict output and charge a price above marginal cost. Monopoly generally creates deadweight loss compared with the competitive benchmark.
- External costs such as pollution cause market output to exceed the efficient level; external benefits such as vaccination can cause market output to fall below the efficient level. Corrective taxes, subsidies, property rights, or regulation can address some externalities.
- Public goods are nonrival and nonexcludable, creating free-rider problems. Common resources are rival but difficult to exclude users from, creating overuse risks.
- Rent-seeking uses resources to obtain transfers or protected privileges rather than create productive value. Government failure and unintended incentives matter in policy analysis.

## Required problem-solving habits

1. Define the variable or concept before calculating.
2. Separate a movement along a curve from a shift of the curve.
3. Label units and distinguish totals, averages, marginal changes, levels, and percentage changes.
4. State the relevant ceteris paribus assumption.
5. For a graph, identify axes, initial equilibrium, the shifted curve, and the new equilibrium.
6. For a table, calculate changes between adjacent rows before calculating averages or marginal values.
7. For policy questions, identify the market failure or macroeconomic objective, then analyze benefits, costs, and unintended effects.
8. Check that the selected answer, numerical work, explanation, and diagram all agree.
